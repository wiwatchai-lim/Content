## A. Perceptual organisation: Gestalt and figure-ground

### Wertheimer1923
- Title: Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II (English: "Laws of organization in perceptual forms")
- Author: Max Wertheimer. Year: 1923. Venue: Psychologische Forschung 4, 301–350. English translation by W. Ellis in
  *A Source Book of Gestalt Psychology* (1938), pp. 71–88, Routledge & Kegan Paul. A newer translation is in
  Spillmann (ed.), *On Perceived Motion and Figural Organization*, MIT Press 2012 (listing only).
- URL: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms.htm
- Evidence type: theory plus demonstrations (classic primary source).
- Accessed: full text of the Ellis 1938 translation (York University "Classics in the History of Psychology").
- Findings: (1) Grouping factors are named and shown with dot and line figures: proximity, similarity, "uniform
  destiny" (common fate), direction and "good curve" (good continuation), closure, Prägnanz (stable "good" figures),
  past experience, and "objective set" (what came before biases how a later display groups). (2) Wertheimer argues
  that past experience exists as a factor but cannot explain all organisation.
- Limitations: demonstrations, not measured experiments; the factors interact and can conflict, and the paper gives no
  rule for how much each one weighs.

### Wagemans2012
- Title: A century of Gestalt psychology in visual perception: I. Perceptual grouping and figure-ground organization
- Authors: J. Wagemans, J. H. Elder, M. Kubovy, S. E. Palmer, M. A. Peterson, M. Singh, R. von der Heydt. Year: 2012.
  Venue: Psychological Bulletin 138(6), 1172–1217.
- DOI: 10.1037/a0029333 (PubMed 22845751; PMC3482144)
- Evidence type: review.
- Accessed: abstract and indexing records (PubMed, repository listings).
- Findings: (1) Covers the classic grouping principles (proximity, similarity, common fate, good continuation) plus
  newer ones such as common region and synchrony, and their role in contour integration and completion. (2) Reviews
  image-based figure-ground cues, how past experience and attention shape figure-ground, and how it links to shape
  and depth perception. (3) Reviews neural work on border ownership.
- Limitations: abstract only was read. Part II (Wagemans et al. 2012, conceptual foundations, PMC3728284) was not
  read.

### Vecera2002
- Title: Lower region: a new cue for figure-ground assignment
- Authors: S. P. Vecera, E. K. Vogel, G. F. Woodman. Year: 2002. Venue: Journal of Experimental Psychology: General
  131(2), 194–205.
- DOI: 10.1037/0096-3445.131.2.194 (PubMed 12049239)
- Evidence type: empirical (eight experiments).
- Accessed: abstract and the APA press summary.
- Findings: (1) The lower region of a display is more likely to be seen as figure than the upper region. (2) The effect
  was not explained by contrast, eye movements or voluntary attention, and "lower" was defined relative to the display.
  (3) The authors suggest it reflects scene statistics: what is below the horizon is usually nearer.
- Limitations: simple two-region lab displays; size of the effect in rich pictures is not known.

### Cavanagh2005
- Title: The artist as neuroscientist
- Author: Patrick Cavanagh. Year: 2005. Venue: Nature 434, 301–307.
- DOI: 10.1038/434301a
- Evidence type: review/essay drawing on experiments.
- Accessed: full text (course-hosted PDF copy of the published article).
- Findings: (1) Viewers do not notice many departures from real physics: shadows can have the wrong colour, shape or
  direction, but they must be darker than their surround and must not look opaque, or the 3D shape is lost. (2)
  Inconsistent lighting direction is hard to spot: in an array of cubes lit from one direction, observers took a long
  time to find the one lit differently. (3) Line drawings work because artists draw the contours that define shape,
  not cast-shadow outlines or pigment edges; infants and monkeys can read line drawings. (4) Transparency needs aligned
  "X-junctions" (Metelli's work): the edge of the transparent thing must cross the edges behind it; refraction can be
  wrong without being noticed. (5) For mirrors and reflections, "almost any reflection will do" as long as it reads as
  a reflection.
- Limitations: an essay; several claims rest on cited studies that were not read here.

## B. Depth, perspective and atmosphere

### CuttingVishton1995
- Title: Perceiving layout and knowing distances: the integration, relative potency, and contextual use of different
  information about depth
- Authors: J. E. Cutting, P. M. Vishton. Year: 1995. Venue: chapter in W. Epstein & S. Rogers (eds.), *Handbook of
  Perception and Cognition*, Vol. 5, *Perception of Space and Motion*, pp. 69–117, Academic Press.
- Evidence type: review/theory chapter.
- Accessed: bibliographic only, plus descriptions in citing papers (search results).
- Findings (from citing sources): (1) Compares how useful each depth cue is with distance and proposes three zones:
  personal space, action space and vista space. (2) Occlusion gives only depth order (ordinal), but works at all
  distances. (3) Cues differ in precision and are weighted differently when combined; height in the visual field is an
  effective distance cue.
- Limitations: content known only second-hand; no figures or exact functions were read.

### OShea1994
- Title: Contrast as a depth cue
- Authors: R. P. O'Shea, S. G. Blackburn, H. Ono. Year: 1994. Venue: Vision Research 34(12), 1595–1604.
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: abstract (university repository records).
- Findings: (1) Of two areas, the lower-contrast one was judged farther away. (2) The effect held when a size cue
  pointed the other way, and with no other depth information present. (3) The authors propose contrast as a pictorial
  cue that imitates aerial perspective.
- Limitations: simple monitor stimuli; sample size and effect size not read.

### Britannica-AerialPerspective
- Title: "aerial perspective" (encyclopedia entry). Publisher: Encyclopaedia Britannica.
- URL: https://www.britannica.com/art/aerial-perspective
- Evidence type: encyclopedia.
- Accessed: entry excerpt via search results (not fully read).
- Findings: (1) Attributes the term "aerial perspective" to Leonardo da Vinci's *Treatise on Painting*. (2) Physics
  context from the same search (Wikipedia, low-grade source): scattering adds a veiling sky light to distant objects,
  lowering their contrast with the sky across all spatial frequencies (unlike blur, which removes fine detail);
  scattering by air molecules (Rayleigh) is wavelength-dependent, which gives distance its blue cast, while haze
  particles (Mie) scatter more evenly and give grey-white veils.
- Limitations: encyclopedia level only; no primary optics paper or Leonardo passage was read. Use with OShea1994.

### Smarthistory-Perspective
- Title: Linear Perspective: Brunelleschi's Experiment (video and transcript). Publisher: Smarthistory.
- URL: https://smarthistory.org/linear-perspective
- Evidence type: course page (art history).
- Accessed: page excerpt via search results.
- Findings: (1) Brunelleschi showed around 1420 (some sources say 1413) at the Florence Baptistery that a flat painted
  panel could read as convincing 3D space; a panel with a peephole and a mirror let the viewer compare painting and
  building from one point. (2) About two decades later Alberti codified the method in *On Painting* (*De pictura*,
  c. 1435).
- Limitations: the panel is lost, so the exact construction is unknown; dates differ between sources. The
  "costruzione legittima" link is a hypothesis (Brown University course page), not fact.

### Kubovy1986
- Title: The Psychology of Perspective and Renaissance Art
- Author: Michael Kubovy. Year: 1986 (paperback 1988). Publisher: Cambridge University Press.
- Evidence type: textbook/theory with experiments.
- Accessed: bibliographic only. Secondary: Wikipedia entry on Kubovy, and the WebExhibits pages "The Arrow in the
  Eye" (https://www.webexhibits.org/arrowintheeye/robustness2.html); the fetched page did not show its authorship,
  so it is not counted as the book.
- Findings (secondary): (1) "Robustness of perspective": pictures still look right from many viewpoints, as long as
  the viewer can see the picture surface as a surface (the effect fails for illusionistic ceilings). (2) Seen far
  from the correct viewpoint, objects near the picture edge look distorted ("marginal distortions"); the WebExhibits
  page says Renaissance painters avoided motifs that seem to follow the viewer, such as pointing fingers or gun
  barrels aimed out.
- Limitations: book not read; wording of secondary pages not checked against it.

## C. Composition, balance, framing and the "rules"

### Arnheim1974
- Title: Art and Visual Perception: A Psychology of the Creative Eye (new version, expanded and revised)
- Author: Rudolf Arnheim. Year: 1974 (first ed. 1954). Publisher: University of California Press, Berkeley. ISBN
  9780520026131 (50th anniversary printing 9780520243835).
- URL: https://www.ucpress.edu/books/art-and-visual-perception-second-edition/paper
- Evidence type: theory (practitioner and psychology book).
- Accessed: bibliographic only (UC Press page, library catalogues).
- Findings (secondary): WilsonChatterjee2005 cite Arnheim (his 1988 *The Power of the Center*, not this book) for the
  claim that balance is a central feature of aesthetic images. Locher1998 test theoretical ideas about pictorial
  balance of this tradition. No content from the 1974 book itself is cited here.
- Limitations: not read. Arnheim's ideas are theory; treat "visual weight" claims as hypotheses to test.

### Locher1998
- Title: The role of balance as an organizing design principle underlying adults' compositional strategies for
  creating visual displays
- Authors: P. J. Locher, P. J. Stappers, K. Overbeeke. Year: 1998. Venue: Acta Psychologica 99(2), 141–161.
- DOI: 10.1016/S0001-6918(98)00008-0
- Evidence type: empirical (production task).
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) Adults making abstract displays in a square field kept the design's weighted centre close to the
  geometric centre of the field at every stage of construction. (2) The field centre acts as a balancing anchor.
- Limitations: abstract only; participants were not trained artists; abstract elements, not pictures.

### McManus1985
- Title: Balance in pictures
- Authors: I. C. McManus, D. Edmondson, J. Rodger. Year: 1985. Venue: British Journal of Psychology 76(3), 311–324.
- DOI: 10.1111/j.2044-8295.1985.tb01955.x
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: bibliographic record and descriptions in citing papers.
- Findings (secondary): (1) Participants placed a fulcrum under artworks and plain stimuli where each looked balanced;
  balance points fell near the frame centre. (2) Object position mattered most; size and colour had some effect.
- Limitations: content second-hand.

### WilsonChatterjee2005
- Title: The assessment of preference for balance: introducing a new test
- Authors: A. Wilson, A. Chatterjee. Year: 2005. Venue: Empirical Studies of the Arts 23(2), 165–180.
- URL: https://neuroaesthetics.med.upenn.edu/assets/user-content/documents/publications/wilson-chatterjee-2005-01.pdf
- Evidence type: empirical (test construction).
- Accessed: full text.
- Findings: (1) An objective balance score (area difference across 8 axes: vertical, horizontal, two diagonals,
  inner/outer) explained 71% of variance in rated balance. (2) In the final test, it explained 73% (circles) and 78%
  (hexagons) of variance in preference, 44% for squares. (3) Preference plateaued at an imbalance of about 20%:
  beyond moderate balance, more symmetry did not raise liking.
- Limitations: abstract dot patterns, untrained participants; later studies (cited in Gershoni2011) found weaker links
  between this score and liking.

### Gershoni2011
- Title: Measuring pictorial balance perception at first glance using Japanese calligraphy
- Authors: S. Gershoni, S. Hochstein. Year: 2011. Venue: i-Perception 2(6), 508–527.
- DOI: 10.1068/i0472 (PMC3485800)
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) Balance ratings and balance comparisons agreed well with each other but poorly with the APB area
  score. (2) Rotating images left the area score unchanged but changed ratings strongly, especially for art experts.
  (3) The authors propose that first-glance balance depends on global organisation (grouping of strokes, object
  recognition, preference for horizontals/verticals, vertical symmetry), not just element size and position.
- Limitations: calligraphy stimuli; abstract only.

### Palmer2008
- Title: Aesthetic issues in spatial composition: effects of position and direction on framing single objects
- Authors: S. E. Palmer, J. S. Gardner, T. D. Wickens. Year: 2008. Venue: Spatial Vision 21, 421–449.
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: bibliographic record; content via PalmerLab-Spatial and citing papers.
- Findings (secondary): (1) Centre bias: front-facing objects are preferred at the frame centre. (2) Inward bias:
  objects facing right are preferred left of centre and vice versa, so they face into the frame.
- Limitations: single objects on plain grounds; content second-hand.

### PalmerLab-Spatial
- Title: Spatial aesthetics research page ("How the 'Rule of Thirds' is Wrong: Let us Count the ways")
- Publisher: Palmer Lab, UC Berkeley. URL: https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html
- Evidence type: lab web page summarising papers and conference posters.
- Accessed: full page.
- Findings: (1) In Palmer, Hara & Griscom's rule-of-thirds test, the most preferred position for every object and
  facing direction was the frame centre; predicted peaks on third lines and dips on the axes were not found. (2)
  Vertical bias follows the world: objects supported from below are preferred lower, from above higher; an eagle is
  preferred higher, a stingray lower; objects seen from below are preferred higher. (3) Preferred size in frame
  scales with the log of real object size (small things small, big things big; Konkle & Oliva posters). (4) Inward
  bias weakens as implied speed rises, and for divers motion direction beats facing direction. (5) Unusual
  compositions are preferred when context justifies them ("representational fit"); violating defaults can add value
  when it serves meaning.
- Limitations: much of it is poster abstracts without statistics; the page itself says the rule may still help artists
  by encouraging off-centre placement that works in context.

### Amirshahi2014
- Title: Evaluating the rule of thirds in photographs and paintings
- Authors: S. A. Amirshahi, G. U. Hayn-Leichsenring, J. Denzler, C. Redies. Year: 2014. Venue: Art & Perception 2,
  163–182.
- DOI: 10.1163/22134913-00002024 (open access, CC BY-NC)
- Evidence type: empirical + computational.
- Accessed: full text.
- Findings: (1) Saliency-based ROT metrics matched human ROT judgements only partly. (2) Aesthetic ratings correlated
  only weakly with subjective ROT scores and not at all with computed ROT values. (3) Highly rated photographs and a
  large set of paintings scored about as low on ROT as photos that do not follow it: "the rule of thirds seems to play
  only a minor, if any, role in large sets of high-quality photographs and paintings."
- Limitations: depends on saliency models to locate the focus; paintings had no aesthetic ratings.

### Tyler1998
- Title: Painters centre one eye in portraits
- Author: C. W. Tyler. Year: 1998. Venue: Nature 392, 877–878.
- URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/31833
- Evidence type: empirical (corpus analysis of about 265 portraits, per a later review).
- Accessed: abstract; secondary (later review, press).
- Findings: (1) Across about 600 years of portraits, one eye tends to sit near the horizontal centre of the canvas. (2)
  A 2019 follow-up (Bruno, Bertamini & Tyler, PLOS ONE) found Instagram selfies often centre one eye too (listing
  only).
- Limitations: critics argue it may follow from fitting a head in a frame, not a hidden aesthetic rule.

## D. Proportion and the golden section

### McManus1980
- Title: The aesthetics of simple figures
- Author: I. C. McManus. Year: 1980. Venue: British Journal of Psychology 71, 505–524.
- Evidence type: review + empirical.
- Accessed: abstract (Scilit) and secondary quotes (Plus magazine).
- Findings: (1) Earlier rectangle studies had method problems, possible experimenter bias, and ignored individual
  differences. (2) Individual preferences were consistent over time but differed between people, and loaded on two
  factors: one around the square, one near the golden section. (3) He found "moderately good evidence" for the
  Fechner effect but doubted whether the exact ratio matters rather than nearby ratios such as 1.5–1.6 (per Plus).
- Limitations: simple rectangles, not pictures.

### Green1995
- Title: All that glitters: a review of psychological research on the aesthetics of the golden section
- Author: C. D. Green. Year: 1995. Venue: Perception 24, 937–968.
- DOI: 10.1068/p240937. Author page: https://www.yorku.ca/christo/papers/goldrev3.htm
- Evidence type: review.
- Accessed: bibliographic record and author landing page only (the full PDF was not read).
- Findings (secondary): a citing study notes Green showed that Fechner's procedures and findings were often
  misrepresented by later researchers.
- Limitations: no conclusions of the review are claimed here beyond that.

## E. Attention, saliency and how people look at pictures

### Yarbus1967
- Title: Eye Movements and Vision. Author: A. L. Yarbus. Year: 1967 (English translation). Publisher: Plenum Press.
- Evidence type: empirical monograph.
- Accessed: bibliographic only; content via Tatler2010.
- Findings (secondary, Tatler2010): his recordings, including the same painting viewed under different instructions,
  showed that the viewer's task changes where people look; his figures are in most perception textbooks.
- Limitations: few observers; not read.

### Tatler2010
- Title: Yarbus, eye movements, and vision
- Authors: B. W. Tatler, N. J. Wade, H. Kwan, J. M. Findlay, B. M. Velichkovsky. Year: 2010. Venue: i-Perception 1(1),
  7–27.
- DOI: 10.1068/i0382 (PMC3563050)
- Evidence type: review + empirical replication.
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) Biography and impact of Yarbus. (2) Revisits and extends his task-instruction study; task effects on
  viewing are confirmed.
- Limitations: abstract only.

### DeAngelusPelz2009
- Title: Top-down control of eye movements: Yarbus revisited
- Authors: M. DeAngelus, J. B. Pelz. Year: 2009. Venue: Visual Cognition 17(6/7), 790–811.
- Evidence type: empirical replication.
- Accessed: bibliographic only; described in citing reviews as a modern replication of task effects.
- Findings: none cited beyond "replicates Yarbus's task effect with modern eye tracking" (secondary).
- Limitations: not read.

### Greene2012
- Title: Reconsidering Yarbus: a failure to predict observers' task from eye movement patterns
- Authors: M. R. Greene, T. Liu, J. M. Wolfe. Year: 2012. Venue: Vision Research.
- DOI: 10.1016/j.visres.2012.03.019 (PubMed 22487718)
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) A classifier could tell which image and which observer from scan paths, but not which task; human
  judges failed too. (2) Static scan paths alone are not enough to infer mental state. Later papers (Borji & Itti 2014,
  see Unverified) report task decoding slightly above chance.
- Limitations: this does not say task has no effect on gaze; only that the effect is hard to decode.

### Itti1998
- Title: A model of saliency-based visual attention for rapid scene analysis
- Authors: L. Itti, C. Koch, E. Niebur. Year: 1998. Venue: IEEE TPAMI 20(11), 1254–1259.
- DOI: 10.1109/34.730558
- Evidence type: computational model.
- Accessed: abstract and library records.
- Findings: (1) Colour, intensity and orientation contrast at several scales are combined into one saliency map. (2)
  Attention visits locations in order of decreasing saliency. For art direction: local contrast in colour, value and
  orientation pulls the eye before meaning does.
- Limitations: bottom-up only; ignores faces, text, meaning and task.

### Parkhurst2002
- Title: Modeling the role of salience in the allocation of overt visual attention
- Authors: D. Parkhurst, K. Law, E. Niebur. Year: 2002. Venue: Vision Research 42(1), 107–123.
- DOI: 10.1016/S0042-6989(01)00250-4
- Evidence type: empirical + model.
- Accessed: abstract and listings.
- Findings: the saliency model accounted in part for where people fixated while freely viewing natural and artificial
  scenes; described by later reviews as the first test of the saliency model against human eye movements.
- Limitations: "in part"; later work (Tatler and others) criticises salience-only accounts.

### Tatler2007
- Title: The central fixation bias in scene viewing: selecting an optimal viewing position independently of motor
  biases and image feature distributions
- Author: B. W. Tatler. Year: 2007. Venue: Journal of Vision 7(14):4.
- DOI: 10.1167/7.14.4
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: bibliographic record and descriptions in citing papers.
- Findings (secondary): viewers fixate the centre of a scene strongly, largely independent of content and task, and
  about as strongly when salient features are in the centre or the periphery.
- Limitations: content second-hand; screen viewing in a lab.

### Henderson2003
- Title: Human gaze control during real-world scene perception
- Author: J. M. Henderson. Year: 2003. Venue: Trends in Cognitive Sciences 7(11), 498–504.
- Evidence type: review.
- Accessed: listing and excerpts in citing sources.
- Findings: gaze is both pulled by visual properties of a region and pushed by knowledge and goals; meaning matters
  alongside salience.
- Limitations: excerpts only.

### Quiroga2011
- Title: How do we see art: an eye-tracker study
- Authors: R. Quian Quiroga, C. Pedreira. Year: 2011. Venue: Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 5:98.
- DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2011.00098
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) Gaze on paintings (and digitally altered versions) shared tendencies toward salient regions. (2) For
  figurative works, gaze varied with the viewer's taste and background knowledge; viewing order (original or altered
  first) changed the patterns.
- Limitations: small sample; abstract only.

### Massaro2012
- Title: When art moves the eyes: a behavioral and eye-tracking study
- Authors: D. Massaro, F. Savazzi, C. Di Dio, D. Freedberg, V. Gallese, G. Gilli, A. Marchetti. Year: 2012.
  Venue: PLoS ONE 7(5): e37285.
- DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0037285
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) When a human figure was in the painting, content-driven (top-down) viewing beat low-level salience. (2)
  In nature scenes, low-level features had more influence on gaze.
- Limitations: viewers without art training; representational paintings only.

### Nodine1993
- Title: The role of formal art training on perception and aesthetic judgment of art compositions
- Authors: C. F. Nodine, P. J. Locher, E. A. Krupinski. Year: 1993. Venue: Leonardo 26(3), 219–227.
- URL: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/606664/summary
- Evidence type: empirical.
- Accessed: listing and descriptions in citing reviews.
- Findings (secondary): untrained viewers fixate more on central and foreground figures; art-trained viewers spend
  longer on background and structural features.
- Limitations: small sample (as noted by reviewers); not read.

## F. Value, colour-luminance, edges and texture

### Livingstone2002
- Title: Vision and Art: The Biology of Seeing. Author: Margaret Livingstone. Year: 2002. Publisher: Harry N. Abrams.
- Evidence type: textbook (neuroscience for artists).
- Accessed: bibliographic only; content via Dupree2003.
- Findings: see Dupree2003.
- Limitations: book not read; the "What/Where" split is her simplification of two pathways.

### Dupree2003
- Title: The Neurobiology of Art. Author: Catherine Dupree. Year: 2003. Venue: Harvard Magazine (July–August).
- URL: https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2003/07/the-neurobiology-of-art-html
- Evidence type: science journalism summarising Livingstone2002.
- Accessed: full text.
- Findings: (1) A luminance-based "Where" system handles depth, motion and position and is colour-blind; a "What"
  system handles colour, objects and faces. (2) Shading a circle in light and dark makes it look 3D; the same circle
  in colours of equal luminance looks flat. (3) Equiluminant areas (Monet's sun in *Impression, Sunrise*; Mondrian's
  yellow and grey squares) have poorly defined position and may seem to shimmer or move.
- Limitations: popular summary of a book.

### PublicDomainReview-Dow / Dow1899
- Title: *Composition* (first published 1899; Project Gutenberg #45410 is the 1914 ninth edition, Doubleday, Page).
  Author: Arthur Wesley Dow. Secondary page: "Arthur Wesley Dow's Floating World: Composition (1905 edition)",
  The Public Domain Review, https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/dow-composition/
- Evidence type: practitioner book (design pedagogy); museum-style essay.
- Accessed: Gutenberg listing (text download returned HTTP 503, not read); Public Domain Review page read in full.
- Findings (secondary): (1) Dow organised teaching around line, notan (light-dark arrangement, without colour,
  texture and detail) and colour. (2) "Notan" was adopted from Ernest Fenollosa; Dow's approach drew on Japanese
  woodblock prints. (3) The book shifted classrooms from copying masters to building images from relations of line,
  value and pattern.
- Limitations: book text not read; no exercise details claimed.

### Julesz1981
- Title: Textons, the elements of texture perception, and their interactions
- Author: B. Julesz. Year: 1981. Venue: Nature 290, 91–97.
- DOI: 10.1038/290091a0
- Evidence type: empirical/theory.
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) Preattentive texture segregation depends on a few local features ("textons": e.g. elongated blobs,
  terminators, crossings) and their density. (2) The relative arrangement (phase) of textons is hard to see without
  focused attention.
- Limitations: synthetic textures.

### GrahamRedies2010
- Title: Statistical regularities in art: relations with visual coding and perception
- Authors: D. J. Graham, C. Redies. Year: 2010. Venue: Vision Research (PubMed 20580643).
- Evidence type: review (minireview).
- Accessed: abstract and descriptions in citing papers.
- Findings: (1) Artworks from Western and Eastern traditions show Fourier amplitude spectra falling with spatial
  frequency at a slope near −2 in power, similar to natural scenes. (2) Art statistics may be shaped by efficient
  coding in the visual system.
- Limitations: volume and pages not confirmed; statistical regularity is not the same as quality.

## G. Empirical aesthetics models

### PalmerSchloss2013
- Title: Visual aesthetics and human preference
- Authors: S. E. Palmer, K. B. Schloss, J. Sammartino. Year: 2013. Venue: Annual Review of Psychology 64, 77–107.
- DOI: 10.1146/annurev-psych-120710-100504
- Evidence type: review.
- Accessed: abstract and citing descriptions.
- Findings: (1) Reviews aesthetic preference for colour, spatial composition and shape from definitional, method,
  empirical and theory angles. (2) Argues that aesthetic response can be studied rigorously; methods include
  forced-choice, ranking and rating.
- Limitations: abstract only; specific findings are taken from PalmerLab-Spatial and Palmer2008 instead.

### Leder2004
- Title: A model of aesthetic appreciation and aesthetic judgments
- Authors: H. Leder, B. Belke, A. Oeberst, D. Augustin. Year: 2004. Venue: British Journal of Psychology 95(4),
  489–508.
- DOI: 10.1348/0007126042369811
- Evidence type: theory (information-processing model).
- Accessed: abstract.
- Findings: (1) Five stages: perceptual analysis, implicit memory integration, explicit classification (style,
  content), cognitive mastering (interpretation), evaluation. (2) Two outputs: aesthetic emotion and aesthetic
  judgement. (3) Context and expertise change processing.
- Limitations: built for modern art in museum contexts; a framework, not a measured effect.

## Synthesis for an AI art director

1. Centre is a strong default, not a mistake: viewers prefer a single object at the frame centre, and the rule of
   thirds' predicted peaks were not found. Put the hero (e.g. glasses on a plain ground) centred unless there is a
   reason not to. [finding: PalmerLab-Spatial, Palmer2008]
2. Rule of thirds has weak support as an aesthetic law: high-quality photos and paintings follow it no more than
   photos that ignore it, and ratings barely relate to it. Use it as one option for off-centre placement, never as a
   quality check. [finding: Amirshahi2014, PalmerLab-Spatial]
3. Golden ratio evidence is mixed and weak: rectangle preference varies by person (square vs near-golden factors), old
   studies had method problems, and the exact 1.618 ratio is not shown to beat nearby ratios. Do not sell golden
   spirals as proof of beauty; treat them as a layout grid. [finding: McManus1980, Green1995]
4. Off-centre subjects should face or move into the frame (inward bias); the effect weakens with fast motion, where
   direction of travel rules. [finding: Palmer2008, PalmerLab-Spatial]
5. Place things where they live in the world: supported objects lower, hanging or flying ones higher, seen-from-below
   subjects higher. Break this only on purpose. [finding: PalmerLab-Spatial]
6. Size in frame should roughly match real size class (small items small in frame) unless detail is the point; for
   product close-ups the "detail" exception applies. [finding: PalmerLab-Spatial]
7. Viewers balance around the frame centre; aim for a weighted centre near the middle, but perfect symmetry is not
   needed — preference plateaus at moderate balance. [finding: Locher1998, McManus1985, WilsonChatterjee2005]
8. Balance is judged globally (grouping, recognisable shapes, vertical/horizontal structure), so check balance on the
   whole picture and in its final orientation, not by element area. [finding: Gershoni2011]
9. Gaze goes to local contrast in value, colour and orientation first; make the hero the point of highest contrast
   and lower contrast elsewhere. [finding: Itti1998, Parkhurst2002]
10. People, faces and meaning override raw salience; a face or hand in a product shot will take attention from the
    product, so place it to lead toward the glasses. [finding: Massaro2012, Henderson2003]
11. Viewers fixate the centre regardless of content; key information (logo, product, text in the HTML layer) is safest
    in the central zone, with edges for secondary content. [finding: Tatler2007]
12. Task and knowledge change where people look, but you cannot read intent from a scan path; design for several
    viewing purposes rather than one assumed path. [finding: Tatler2010, Greene2012, Quiroga2011]
13. Untrained viewers look at central foreground figures; trained viewers read the background and structure. Social
    feeds are mostly untrained, fast viewers: keep one clear foreground hero. [finding: Nodine1993]
14. Group by proximity, similarity and common fate: cluster related items, separate unrelated ones with space, and
    give a set of variants one shared treatment. [finding: Wertheimer1923, Wagemans2012]
15. Lower regions read as figure and nearer; a ground plane at the bottom supports a standing subject, and an inverted
    layout feels unstable. [finding: Vecera2002]
16. Depth comes from stacked cues: occlusion for order, height in field and size for distance, contrast and haze for
    far space. Use at least two that agree. [finding: CuttingVishton1995, OShea1994]
17. Push distance with lower contrast and a cool or grey veil; keep the highest contrast in the foreground subject.
    [finding: OShea1994, Britannica-AerialPerspective]
18. Value carries form and depth; colour of equal luminance flattens and makes edges shimmer. Check every image in
    greyscale: the hero must separate by value, not only by hue. [finding: Dupree2003 (Livingstone2002)]
19. Shadows can be stylised in colour and shape but must be darker than surroundings and transparent, not opaque
    patches. [finding: Cavanagh2005]
20. For glasses and glass, transparency reads when lens edges cross background edges with aligned X-junctions; exact
    refraction and exact reflections do not matter, any plausible reflection works. Use controlled reflections, not
    physically perfect ones. [finding: Cavanagh2005]
21. Good line art draws the contours that define form (silhouette, folds, rims), not every shadow edge; this suits the
    line-art and charcoal styles in the project. [finding: Cavanagh2005]
22. Perspective tolerates many viewing positions, but wide-angle edges distort; keep important shapes (faces, frames)
    away from the corners of wide shots. [finding: Kubovy1986 (secondary)]
23. Plan the value pattern first: a 2–3 value notan sketch of the big shapes before detail. [craft; tradition
    documented in PublicDomainReview-Dow]
24. Natural-image statistics are typical of art; heavy uniform noise or flat textureless AI surfaces can look
    unnatural — add moderate texture at several scales, keep it calmer behind the hero. [finding: GrahamRedies2010,
    Julesz1981; craft for the application]
25. Texture boundaries pop preattentively when local features differ (stroke direction, density); a change of texture
    behind the subject separates it without extra colour. [finding: Julesz1981]
26. Figures: idealised fashion/hero figures are often drawn about 8 heads; average people about 7–7.5. Pick the canon
    by style (cartoon, chibi or realistic) and keep it consistent across a series. [craft: Proko-Proportions,
    Loomis1943]
27. Start figures with gesture (the line of action) and contour before detail. [craft: Nicolaides1941 (secondary)]
28. In a portrait crop, placing one eye near the vertical centre line is a long-standing habit; test it as an
    alternative to thirds. [finding: Tyler1998 — corpus pattern, cause debated]
29. Viewers judge in stages (perception, classification, interpretation, evaluation) and context matters; a caption or
    series context can make an unusual composition read as intended. [finding: Leder2004, LederNadal2014,
    PalmerLab-Spatial]
30. Every rule above is a default to choose by purpose: break centring, placement or balance when tension, unease or
    story calls for it, and say why in the critique. [craft; supported by PalmerLab-Spatial "representational fit"]
