# A — Emotion psychology & speech science (verified sources)

Scope: phonetics basics, IPA, prosody/intonation, stress, rhythm, connected speech, emotional prosody, emotion models,
voice quality, voice production, sarcasm/irony prosody, intonational pragmatics, cross-language recognition, and
voice-acting craft (labelled Artistic).

Verification method (2026-11-24): every DOI was resolved against the Crossref API (title, authors, venue, volume,
pages, year), and abstracts were pulled from OpenAlex / Semantic Scholar or the publisher page. Where an open-access PDF
was available (University of Geneva archive, McGill lab page, eScholarship, ACL Anthology, Cogprints), I read the
relevant sections. The "Accessed" field says exactly what was read. "Finding" lines report only what that source
says; anything else is labelled.

General caveat for this whole file: almost all emotional-prosody data are **acted portrayals**, mostly by Western
(German, English, French, Swedish) actors. None of the emotion-acoustics studies below used Thai emotional speech,
except Kumwapee & Jitwiriyanont 2020 (Thai sarcasm, n = 4). Treat every pattern as a tendency, never a rule.

## 1. Brief keys (verified)

### BanseScherer1996
- **Title:** Acoustic profiles in vocal emotion expression
- **Authors:** Rainer Banse, Klaus R. Scherer
- **Year / venue:** 1996, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 70(3), 614–636
- **DOI:** 10.1037/0022-3514.70.3.614 (Crossref confirmed). OA scan: https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:102055
- **Type:** empirical-acted
- **Accessed:** abstract; the OA PDF is an image scan, of which I read the method pages visually (pp. 617–618). Design
  details below come from the abstract and from Scherer2003 and BanzigerScherer2005, which describe this corpus.
- **Findings:**
  1. Professional actors portrayed 14 emotions (224 portrayals analysed). Ten of these were five emotion families each
     split into a low- and a high-arousal member (e.g., cold vs. hot anger, anxiety vs. panic fear, sadness vs. despair,
     happiness vs. elation). Vocal parameters index **intensity/arousal and also valence/quality** differences (abstract).
  2. A set of about 9–10 measures (mean F0, F0 SD, mean energy, duration of voiced periods, proportion of energy below
     1000 Hz, spectral drop-off) explained much of the listeners' judgments (as summarised in Scherer2003, Table 3).
  3. The authors argue that most emotional speech is shaped both by "push" effects (physiology) and by "pull" effects
     (social display conventions), and that only brief "affect bursts" are close to pure push (pp. 617–618, read visually).
- **Limitations:** German-speaking actors; meaningless syllable sentences; acted speech.

### JuslinLaukka2003
- **Title:** Communication of emotions in vocal expression and music performance: Different channels, same code?
- **Authors:** Patrik N. Juslin, Petri Laukka
- **Year / venue:** 2003, Psychological Bulletin 129(5), 770–814
- **DOI:** 10.1037/0033-2909.129.5.770
- **Type:** review / meta-analysis (104 vocal-expression studies, 41 music-performance studies)
- **Accessed:** abstract only (full-text cue tables not accessed)
- **Findings:**
  1. Discrete emotions are communicated with similar accuracy in speech and in music performance.
  2. Emotion-specific cue patterns (rate/tempo, intensity, F0/pitch level, variability, high-frequency energy) are
     shared by speech and music, and they are consistent with Scherer's (1986) predictions.
- **Limitations:** Mostly acted, Western stimuli. I did not read the per-emotion cue table, so it is not reproduced here.

### Scherer2003
- **Title:** Vocal communication of emotion: A review of research paradigms
- **Authors:** Klaus R. Scherer
- **Year / venue:** 2003, Speech Communication 40(1–2), 227–256
- **DOI:** 10.1016/S0167-6393(02)00084-5. OA: https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:102083
- **Type:** review
- **Accessed:** full text (OA PDF); Table 1 was checked visually
- **Findings:**
  1. **Table 1** (synthesis of Johnstone & Scherer 2000), with ↑ = increase and ↓ = decrease:
     - **Stress:** intensity ↑, F0 floor/mean ↑.
     - **Anger/rage:** intensity ↑, F0 mean ↑, F0 variability ↑, F0 range ↑, sentence contours ↓ (falling),
       high-frequency energy ↑, speech/articulation rate ↑.
     - **Fear/panic:** intensity ↑, F0 mean ↑, F0 range ↑ (↓ in some studies), high-frequency energy ↑, rate ↑.
     - **Sadness:** everything ↓ (intensity, F0 mean, variability, range, falling contours, high-frequency energy, rate).
     - **Joy/elation:** intensity ↑, F0 mean ↑, F0 variability ↑, F0 range ↑, (high-frequency energy ↑), (rate ↑).
     - **Boredom:** F0 variability ↓, F0 range ↓, rate ↓.
  2. Much of this consistency reflects **arousal**. Scherer suggests that F0, energy and rate mostly mark arousal, while
     valence and quality differences may show more in **voice source and articulation** (spectrum, formants).
  3. Emotion families matter: hot anger (explosive) and cold anger (controlled) differ acoustically, and failing to say
     which kind was portrayed explains non-replications. Vocal recognition by listeners is about 55–65% accurate
     (5–6× chance), against about 75% for faces.
- **Limitations:** Review of mostly acted data.

### Russell1980
- **Title:** A circumplex model of affect
- **Authors:** James A. Russell
- **Year / venue:** 1980, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 39(6), 1161–1178
- **DOI:** 10.1037/h0077714
- **Type:** theory + empirical (self-report scaling)
- **Accessed:** full text p. 1 (abstract) from an OA scan
- **Findings:**
  1. Affect concepts fall on a circle in a 2-D space of **pleasure–displeasure × arousal–sleep**, in this order:
     pleasure 0°, excitement 45°, arousal 90°, distress 135°, displeasure 180°, depression 225°, sleepiness 270°,
     relaxation 315°.
  2. The circle was supported by four scalings of 28 emotion adjectives (one used 343 subjects' self-reports).
- **Use:** It gives a valence × arousal placement for each emotion entry. Arousal is the axis with the strongest vocal
  correlates (see Scherer2003).
- **Limitations:** English adjectives, self-report. It is a model of affect concepts, not of the voice.

### Mehrabian1996
- **Title:** Pleasure-arousal-dominance: A general framework for describing and measuring individual differences in
  temperament
- **Authors:** Albert Mehrabian
- **Year / venue:** 1996, Current Psychology 14(4), 261–292
- **DOI:** 10.1007/BF02686918
- **Type:** theory / review
- **Accessed:** Crossref metadata plus a partial abstract seen through search snippets (the Springer page was paywalled)
- **Findings:**
  1. The abstract reviews evidence that three nearly orthogonal dimensions (**pleasure, arousal, dominance**) describe
     emotional states sufficiently, and it defines temperament as a person's average emotional state.
- **Use:** It is the basis of the "dominance" axis in the emotion library. Dominance links to the vocal frequency code
  (Ohala1984), as a theoretical bridge only.
- **Limitations:** Self-report psychometrics, not vocal data.

### Ekman1992
- **Title:** An argument for basic emotions
- **Authors:** Paul Ekman
- **Year / venue:** 1992, Cognition and Emotion 6(3–4), 169–200
- **DOI:** 10.1080/02699939208411068
- **Type:** theory
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Emotions evolved for fundamental life tasks. Each basic emotion has its own signal, physiology and antecedents,
     and all share rapid onset, short duration, unbidden occurrence, automatic appraisal and response coherence.
  2. The paper proposes **emotion families** (cited by Scherer2003), which is why hot and cold anger count as kin.
- **Limitations:** Theoretical; the evidence base is mainly facial.

### Plutchik2001
- **Title:** The nature of emotions
- **Authors:** Robert Plutchik
- **Year / venue:** 2001, American Scientist 89(4), 344
- **DOI:** 10.1511/2001.4.344
- **Type:** theory (popular-science review)
- **Accessed:** Crossref metadata only. The publisher page returned HTTP 503 twice.
- **Findings:** The paper's existence and bibliographic data are verified, but **I did not read its content**. The
  commonly cited content (a wheel of 8 primary emotions with intensity gradations and mixed "dyads") is general
  knowledge and was not checked against this text. Use it only as a naming scheme for mixed emotions, with low
  evidential weight.
- **Limitations:** Not a vocal study.

### CowenKeltner2017
- **Title:** Self-report captures 27 distinct categories of emotion bridged by continuous gradients
- **Authors:** Alan S. Cowen, Dacher Keltner
- **Year / venue:** 2017, PNAS 114(38), E7900–E7909
- **DOI:** 10.1073/pnas.1702247114
- **Type:** empirical-natural (self-report to 2,185 videos)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. People report **27 distinct varieties** of emotional experience. Category labels (e.g., amusement) capture reports
     better than valence and arousal do.
  2. The boundaries are **fuzzy, with smooth gradients** (anxiety → fear → horror → disgust; calmness → aesthetic
     appreciation → awe).
- **Use:** It justifies blended emotions and transition entries in the library.
- **Limitations:** US participants; experience, not vocal expression.

### Cowen2019
- **Title:** Mapping 24 emotions conveyed by brief human vocalization
- **Authors:** Alan S. Cowen, Hillary Anger Elfenbein, Petri Laukka, Dacher Keltner
- **Year / venue:** 2019 (online 2018), American Psychologist 74(6), 698–712
- **DOI:** 10.1037/amp0000399. OA: PMC6586540
- **Type:** empirical-acted (2,032 lab vocal bursts) + empirical-natural (48 real-world bursts)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Non-verbal **vocal bursts** (sighs, gasps, laughs, "mm", "ugh") convey at least **24 distinct emotions**, including
     sympathy and awe.
  2. Recognition is organised more by categories than by valence or arousal, and the categories blend through smooth
     gradients.
- **Use:** Interjections and breaths (Thai เฮ้อ, อุ๊ย, หืม, โห) are a rich emotion channel. Directing the burst separately
  from the line is supported.
- **Limitations:** US English speakers and raters (n = 1,105).

### GoblNiChasaide2003
- **Title:** The role of voice quality in communicating emotion, mood and attitude
- **Authors:** Christer Gobl, Ailbhe Ní Chasaide
- **Year / venue:** 2003, Speech Communication 40(1–2), 189–212
- **DOI:** 10.1016/S0167-6393(02)00082-1
- **Type:** empirical (synthesised stimuli, perception test)
- **Accessed:** full text (an OA copy hosted at perso.lisn.upsaclay.fr): abstract and Discussion
- **Findings:**
  1. One utterance was synthesised in seven qualities (harsh, tense, modal, breathy, whispery, creaky, lax-creaky).
     **Voice quality alone** shifted perceived affect, but there is **no one-to-one mapping**: each quality evokes a
     cluster of attributes.
  2. Tense and harsh voice were heard as **high activation and/or power** (confident, interested, happy, but also
     angry and stressed). Breathy, whispery, creaky and especially **lax-creaky** voice were heard as **low activation**
     (relaxed, content, intimate, friendly, but also sad and bored). Lax-creaky signalled intimacy and sadness better
     than breathy did. There was no support for breathy = anger or breathy = happy.
  3. Voice quality was much more effective for **milder states (moods, attitudes)** than for strong emotions.
- **Limitations:** 12 listeners; Irish English; synthetic single points on continuous dimensions; exploratory (the
  authors' own words).

### BanzigerScherer2005
- **Title:** The role of intonation in emotional expressions
- **Authors:** Tanja Bänziger, Klaus R. Scherer
- **Year / venue:** 2005, Speech Communication 46(3–4), 252–267
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.specom.2005.02.016. OA: https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:96410
- **Type:** empirical-acted
- **Accessed:** full text: abstract, method and conclusions
- **Findings:**
  1. **F0 level and range** vary strongly with the **activation** of the emotion. There is little evidence for
     emotion-specific contour **shapes**.
  2. Details (treated as tentative by the authors):
     - Hot anger, cold anger and elation had a larger **second F0 excursion** in the utterance.
     - Despair and elation showed an **"uptrend"** (F0 climbs and stays high until the final fall). Sadness and quiet
       happiness showed a **"downtrend"** (early peak, gradual decline).
     - Hot anger and elation may have **steeper final falls** than anxiety or happiness.
- **Limitations:** 144 portrayals by 9 German actors (from the Banse & Scherer corpus); nonsense syllables; small n.

### CheangPell2008
- **Title:** The sound of sarcasm
- **Authors:** Henry S. Cheang, Marc D. Pell
- **Year / venue:** 2008, Speech Communication 50(5), 366–381
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.specom.2007.11.003
- **Type:** empirical-acted (attitudes posed by speakers, validated by listeners)
- **Accessed:** full text (a PDF hosted at cs.columbia.edu): abstract and conclusion
- **Findings:**
  1. In English, **sarcasm** (compared with humour, sincerity and neutrality) is most robustly marked by a **lower
     mean F0**.
  2. Compared with sincerity, sarcasm also shows **lower HNR** (noisier, less clear voice) and **less F0 variation (SD)**.
  3. In some contexts, **resonance changes** and a **slower rate** and **narrower F0 range** were supplementary cues.
     Language/text also contributes.
- **Limitations:** Native English speakers only (see CheangPell2009 for Cantonese, where the pattern reverses); posed
  speech.

### Pell2009
- **Title:** Factors in the recognition of vocally expressed emotions: A comparison of four languages
- **Authors:** Marc D. Pell, Silke Paulmann, Chinar Dara, Areej Alasseri, Sonja A. Kotz
- **Year / venue:** 2009, Journal of Phonetics 37(4), 417–435
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.wocn.2009.07.005
- **Type:** empirical-acted (pseudo-utterances)
- **Accessed:** full text (author PDF on the McGill Pell lab site): abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. The languages were **English, German, Hindi and Arabic** (confirmed in the paper; one secondary database wrongly
     lists Spanish). Six emotions plus neutral, 4 speakers per language, native listeners.
  2. All emotions were recognised above chance in every language. **Anger, sadness and fear** were recognised best
     regardless of language. Joy/happiness and disgust were less reliable (anger, sadness and fear are the abstract's
     "best" set).
  3. **Speaker F0 (relative pitch level and variability)** was the key cue in all languages. Basic-emotion prosody shows
     "modal tendencies" that are largely unaffected by language.
- **Limitations:** 4 speakers per language; nonsense speech; no tone language included. Note: the Manchester repository
  listing shows a different title and pages (417–425). The published PDF says 417–435.

### Eyben2016
- **Title:** The Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (GeMAPS) for Voice Research and Affective Computing
- **Authors:** Florian Eyben, Klaus R. Scherer, Björn W. Schuller, Johan Sundberg, Elisabeth André, Carlos Busso,
  Laurence Y. Devillers, Julien Epps, Petri Laukka, Shrikanth S. Narayanan, Khiet P. Truong
- **Year / venue:** 2016, IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing 7(2), 190–202
- **DOI:** 10.1109/TAFFC.2015.2457417
- **Type:** standard (feature set)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. A minimal standard set of voice parameters (implemented in openSMILE), chosen for their link to affective
     physiology, proven value and automatic extractability. It performs well against large brute-force sets.
- **Use:** It is the vocabulary for checking a TTS take objectively (F0, loudness, spectral slope, jitter/shimmer, HNR,
  rate).
- **Limitations:** A measurement standard, not an emotion map.

### Ladd2008
- **Title:** Intonational Phonology (2nd ed.), Cambridge Studies in Linguistics 119
- **Authors:** D. Robert Ladd
- **Year / venue:** 2008, Cambridge University Press. ISBN 9780521861175 (hb), 9780521678360 (pb)
- **URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/intonational-phonology/66DA8DF1174F9B0E01AB3B82E34488DF
- **Type:** textbook / theory
- **Accessed:** publisher page and table-of-contents description (not the book text)
- **Findings:**
  1. The standard reference for **autosegmental-metrical (AM)** intonation: tonal targets (H/L), alignment and scaling,
     sentence stress/focus, prosodic structure. The 2nd edition adds ToBI, pitch alignment and focus.
- **Limitations:** Book not read here. Cite it only for the framework, not for specific claims.

### TingsabadhAbramson1993
- **Title:** Thai (Illustrations of the IPA)
- **Authors:** M. R. Kalaya Tingsabadh, Arthur S. Abramson
- **Year / venue:** 1993, Journal of the International Phonetic Association 23(1), 24–28
- **DOI:** 10.1017/S0025100300004746
- **Type:** standard / descriptive phonetics
- **Accessed:** full text (PDF already present in this session's tool results), introduction and tone section
- **Findings:**
  1. Standard Thai has **five tones**: mid, low, falling, high, rising.
  2. Under **weak stress / casual style**, vowel quantity, tone and glottal stop change. For example, "แต่" (but) becomes
     short /tè/, and จะ loses its glottal stop with a tone change from low to mid. Connected-speech reduction in Thai
     touches tone and length, which matters when directing fast casual Thai.
- **Limitations:** Read-speech illustration with 3 speakers.

## 2. Added sources — emotional prosody & dimensions (verified)

### Scherer1986
- **Title:** Vocal affect expression: A review and a model for future research
- **Authors:** Klaus R. Scherer
- **Year / venue:** 1986, Psychological Bulletin 99(2), 143–165
- **DOI:** 10.1037/0033-2909.99.2.143
- **Type:** review / theory
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. The **component-process model** predicts the voice from appraisal outcomes, via three voice-type dimensions:
     **narrow–wide, lax–tense, full–thin**.
  2. The best-supported dimension is **tense vs. lax** voice. Research had neglected voice quality and the social
     signalling (pull) function.
- **Limitations:** Theory; the review predates modern acoustics.

### JuslinLaukka2001
- **Title:** Impact of intended emotion intensity on cue utilization and decoding accuracy in vocal expression of emotion
- **Authors:** Patrik N. Juslin, Petri Laukka
- **Year / venue:** 2001, Emotion 1(4), 381–412
- **DOI:** 10.1037/1528-3542.1.4.381
- **Type:** empirical-acted
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. **Strong-intensity** portrayals were decoded more accurately than weak ones.
  2. The **same emotion at different intensity uses different cue patterns**.
  3. **F0 and high-frequency energy** best predicted perceived intensity.
- **Use:** Always state the intensity in the direction ("mild irritation" ≠ "fury").
- **Limitations:** Swedish actors, acted speech.

### Laukka2005
- **Title:** A dimensional approach to vocal expression of emotion
- **Authors:** Petri Laukka, Patrik N. Juslin, Roberto Bresin
- **Year / venue:** 2005, Cognition & Emotion 19(5), 633–653
- **DOI:** 10.1080/02699930441000445
- **Type:** empirical-acted
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Activation, valence, potency and intensity ratings all correlated with vocal cues.
  2. Listener ratings could be predicted from cues for **every dimension except valence**.
  3. Intensity correlated with activation.
- **Use:** The voice carries arousal and power well. Valence (pleasant vs. unpleasant) needs text, voice quality and
  context.
- **Limitations:** Acted; 5 emotions.

### GoudbeekScherer2010
- **Title:** Beyond arousal: Valence and potency/control cues in the vocal expression of emotion
- **Authors:** Martijn Goudbeek, Klaus Scherer
- **Year / venue:** 2010, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 128(3), 1322–1336
- **DOI:** 10.1121/1.3466853
- **Type:** empirical-acted (GEMEP corpus, professional actors coached by a stage director)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. The study largely **replicated** Banse & Scherer 1996.
  2. Arousal dominates most parameters, but **spectral balance and spectral noise** relate specifically to **valence
     and potency/control**.
- **Use:** Use timbre/voice quality (brightness, breathiness, roughness), not only pitch and speed, for pleasant vs.
  unpleasant and for in-control vs. not-in-control.
- **Limitations:** French-speaking actors; acted.

### Banziger2012
- **Title:** Introducing the Geneva Multimodal expression corpus for experimental research on emotion perception
- **Authors:** Tanja Bänziger, Marcello Mortillaro, Klaus R. Scherer
- **Year / venue:** 2012, Emotion 12(5), 1161–1179
- **DOI:** 10.1037/a0025827
- **Type:** corpus / standard
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Introduces the GEMEP Core Set: dynamic multimodal portrayals with validation data and micro-coded vocal and
     facial elements.
- **Limitations:** Acted, French-speaking actors.

### MurrayArnott1993
- **Title:** Toward the simulation of emotion in synthetic speech: A review of the literature on human vocal emotion
- **Authors:** Iain R. Murray, John L. Arnott
- **Year / venue:** 1993, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 93(2), 1097–1108
- **DOI:** 10.1121/1.405558
- **Type:** review (aimed at speech synthesis)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Emotion affects three main parameter groups: **voice quality, utterance timing, and pitch contour**.
  2. These can be controlled systematically in synthesis.
- **Use:** It is the historic basis of "emotion by rule" TTS. Note that GoblNiChasaide2003 did *not* confirm some of
  its voice-quality claims (breathy = anger or happiness).
- **Limitations:** Review of 1990-era literature.

### Ohala1984
- **Title:** An ethological perspective on common cross-language utilization of F0 of voice
- **Authors:** John J. Ohala
- **Year / venue:** 1984, Phonetica 41(1), 1–16
- **DOI:** 10.1159/000261706
- **Type:** theory
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. The **frequency code**: high F0 ≈ "small vocaliser" → subordinate, non-threatening, polite, seeking goodwill. Low
     F0 ≈ "large vocaliser" → dominant, assertive, threatening.
  2. It explains cross-language question-rise tendencies and the smile-like mouth shape.
- **Use:** Raise pitch for deference/politeness/cuteness and lower it for authority/menace. Map this to PAD dominance.
- **Limitations:** Theoretical; a tendency.

### Gussenhoven2002
- **Title:** Intonation and interpretation: phonetics and phonology
- **Authors:** Carlos Gussenhoven
- **Year / venue:** 2002, Speech Prosody 2002, 47–57
- **DOI:** 10.21437/speechprosody.2002-7. OA: https://www.isca-archive.org/speechprosody_2002/gussenhoven02_speechprosody.pdf
- **Type:** theory
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. There are three **biological codes**: the **Frequency Code** (Ohala), the **Effort Code** (more effort → wider
     excursions → emphasis/significance) and the **Production Code** (high early, low late in the breath group →
     beginnings vs. endings).
  2. Each code has informational and affective meanings. Languages grammaticalise these meanings differently, but never
     invert the natural form–function relation.
- **Use:** It explains why "bigger pitch movement = more important or more excited" carries over to most languages.
- **Limitations:** Theory.

## 3. Added sources — cross-language / cross-cultural recognition (verified)

### ElfenbeinAmbady2002
- **Title:** On the universality and cultural specificity of emotion recognition: A meta-analysis
- **Authors:** Hillary Anger Elfenbein, Nalini Ambady
- **Year / venue:** 2002, Psychological Bulletin 128(2), 203–235
- **DOI:** 10.1037/0033-2909.128.2.203
- **Type:** meta-analysis (all channels)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Emotions are recognised above chance universally, with an **in-group advantage**.
  2. The advantage is smaller between groups with more mutual exposure.
- **Limitations:** Mostly faces.

### Scherer2001
- **Title:** Emotion inferences from vocal expression correlate across languages and cultures
- **Authors:** Klaus R. Scherer, Rainer Banse, Harald G. Wallbott
- **Year / venue:** 2001, Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology 32(1), 76–92
- **DOI:** 10.1177/0022022101032001009
- **Type:** empirical-acted (perception in 9 countries)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. German actors' language-free portrayals were recognised at 66% overall, ranging from 74% in Germany to 52% in
     **Indonesia** (the only SE Asian sample).
  2. Confusion patterns were similar everywhere.
  3. Accuracy fell as **language dissimilarity** from German increased.
- **Use:** Thai listeners may decode Western-style TTS emotion less precisely. Exaggerated "Western" emotion may read
  oddly.
- **Limitations:** Only 4 emotions plus neutral; one encoder culture.

### Laukka2014
- **Title:** Evidence for cultural dialects in vocal emotion expression: Acoustic classification within and across five
  nations
- **Authors:** Petri Laukka, Daniel Neiberg, Hillary Anger Elfenbein
- **Year / venue:** 2014, Emotion 14(3), 445–449
- **DOI:** 10.1037/a0036048
- **Type:** empirical-acted (machine classification)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. 11 emotions from 100 actors in 5 English-speaking nations were classified above chance across cultures.
  2. Within-culture classification was better, which suggests **cultural "dialects"** of vocal expression.
- **Limitations:** English-speaking cultures only.

### Laukka2016
- **Title:** The expression and recognition of emotions in the voice across five nations: A lens model analysis based on
  acoustic features
- **Authors:** Petri Laukka, Hillary Anger Elfenbein, Nutankumar S. Thingujam, Thomas Rockstuhl, Frederick K. Iraki,
  Wanda Chui
- **Year / venue:** 2016, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 111(5), 686–705
- **DOI:** 10.1037/pspi0000066
- **Type:** empirical-acted
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Eleven states were portrayed: anger, contempt, fear, happiness, interest, lust, neutral, pride, relief, sadness,
     shame. Acoustic cues are used similarly across groups, with **subtle cultural accents**.
  2. The in-group advantage arises from a better match between production and perception schemas.
- **Use:** The pride, relief, interest, contempt and shame categories have acoustic data here (abstract only, no
  directions given).
- **Limitations:** English-language sentences.

### LaukkaElfenbein2021
- **Title:** Cross-cultural emotion recognition and in-group advantage in vocal expression: A meta-analysis
- **Authors:** Petri Laukka, Hillary Anger Elfenbein
- **Year / venue:** 2021 (online Feb 2020), Emotion Review 13(1), 3–11
- **DOI:** 10.1177/1754073919897295
- **Type:** meta-analysis (37 studies; expressers from 26 cultures, perceivers from 44)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Many positive and negative emotions are recognised above chance cross-culturally, with an in-group advantage.
  2. **Greater cultural distance** (Hofstede) → lower accuracy and a larger in-group advantage.
- **Limitations:** Heterogeneous study designs.

### Sauter2010
- **Title:** Cross-cultural recognition of basic emotions through nonverbal emotional vocalizations
- **Authors:** Disa A. Sauter, Frank Eisner, Paul Ekman, Sophie K. Scott
- **Year / venue:** 2010, PNAS 107(6), 2408–2412
- **DOI:** 10.1073/pnas.0908239106 (a correction was published in PNAS 2015, 112(23))
- **Type:** empirical (Western vs. Himba, Namibia)
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. Vocalisations for anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness and surprise were recognised in both directions across
     cultures.
  2. Most **positive** emotions other than joy (e.g., triumph, relief) used **culture-specific** signals. Laughter is
     the notable exception reported in the literature, but that is not in this abstract.
- **Limitations:** Small, specific samples.

## 4. Added sources — sarcasm / irony (verified)

### BryantFoxTree2005
- **Title:** Is there an ironic tone of voice?
- **Authors:** Gregory A. Bryant, Jean E. Fox Tree
- **Year / venue:** 2005, Language and Speech 48(3), 257–277
- **DOI:** 10.1177/00238309050480030101. OA: https://escholarship.org/uc/item/85h6g1ps
- **Type:** empirical-natural (talk-radio speech) + perception experiments
- **Accessed:** full text: abstract and General Discussion
- **Findings:**
  1. Spontaneous ironic speech differed from matched non-ironic speech **only in amplitude variability** (and only for
     the clearest items).
  2. There is **no single ironic tone of voice**. Listeners combine many cues, including context. Speakers layer
     prosody: a "pretend anger" contour over laughter, for example.
- **Use:** Sarcasm direction must come from the scene and intent, not from one preset "sarcastic voice".
- **Limitations:** US English radio speech.

### Rockwell2000
- **Title:** Lower, slower, louder: Vocal cues of sarcasm
- **Authors:** Patricia Rockwell
- **Year / venue:** 2000, Journal of Psycholinguistic Research 29(5), 483–495
- **DOI:** 10.1023/A:1005120109296
- **Type:** empirical (posed and spontaneous sarcasm, perceptual coding)
- **Accessed:** partial abstract through search snippets plus citing papers
- **Findings:**
  1. Perceptual coding found **slower tempo, greater intensity, lower pitch level** for sarcasm (English).
  2. The loudness finding conflicts with other work (BryantFoxTree2005).
- **Limitations:** Perceptual (not instrumental) coding; English.

### CheangPell2009
- **Title:** Acoustic markers of sarcasm in Cantonese and English
- **Authors:** Henry S. Cheang, Marc D. Pell
- **Year / venue:** 2009, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 126(3), 1394–1405
- **DOI:** 10.1121/1.3177275
- **Type:** empirical-acted
- **Accessed:** abstract
- **Findings:**
  1. **Cantonese** sarcasm had a **higher mean F0**, reduced amplitude and F0 range, sometimes a slower rate, and
     **higher HNR**.
  2. English speakers **lowered** F0 for sarcasm, while Cantonese speakers **raised** it. Sarcasm conventions are
     **language-specific**.
- **Use:** Do not transfer the English "low, flat" sarcasm recipe to a tone language without checking.
- **Limitations:** 6 speakers; posed.

### Kumwapee2020
- **Title:** Expressing the Opposite: Acoustic Cues of Thai Verbal Irony
- **Authors:** Nimit Kumwapee, Sujinat Jitwiriyanont
- **Year / venue:** 2020, Proceedings of the 34th Pacific Asia Conference on Language, Information and Computation
  (PACLIC 34)
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2020.paclic-1.50/
- **Type:** empirical-acted (read speech)
- **Accessed:** full text (PDF already present in this session's tool results): abstract and conclusion
- **Findings:**
  1. In **Thai**, read sarcasm (vs. neutral) had a **faster rate, higher F0 mean, wider F0 range and higher amplitude**.
     F0 slope plus intercept together separated the two.
  2. Gender differences: men slowed down and raised F0, women widened F0 range. One speaker instead stressed every
     word and slowed down.
- **Use:** The only Thai acoustic data in this file. It is the opposite of the English pattern, which supports
  language-specific direction.
- **Limitations:** **n = 4 speakers**, read speech, no context control; a conference paper.

## Synthesis for voice direction

1. **Arousal is the strongest and most reliable vocal axis.** Higher arousal → higher F0 level, wider range, louder,
   faster, more high-frequency energy. Lower arousal → the reverse. Direct arousal first.
   [finding: Scherer2003, BanzigerScherer2005, Laukka2005, Pell2009]
2. **Pitch and speed alone do not separate valence.** Pleasant vs. unpleasant and in-control vs. overwhelmed are carried
   more by timbre (spectral balance, noise/breathiness, tension). Say the voice quality explicitly in the style prompt.
   [finding: GoudbeekScherer2010, Laukka2005, Scherer2003]
3. **Use the Table 1 tendencies as a starting map, not a recipe:**
   - anger: loud, high, wide, falling, bright, fast
   - fear/panic: high, fast, bright
   - sadness: everything down
   - joy: up, wide
   - boredom: flat, narrow, slow

   [finding: Scherer2003]
4. **Always name the family member and its intensity.** Cold anger is not hot anger, and despair is not quiet sadness.
   The same emotion at a different intensity uses different cues.
   [finding: Scherer2003, BanseScherer1996, JuslinLaukka2001]
5. **Contour shape matters less than level and range.** If anything, high-arousal states (despair, elation) keep F0
   high until a late, steep final fall ("uptrend"). Calm or sad states peak early and drift down.
   [finding: BanzigerScherer2005; tentative]
6. **Voice quality is the subtle-attitude tool.**
   - Tense/pressed reads as high activation or power (confident, angry, stressed).
   - Lax-creaky or breathy reads as low activation (intimate, relaxed, sad, bored).
   - It works best for mild moods and attitudes, which suits commercial VO warmth and cartoon "soft" lines.

   [finding: GoblNiChasaide2003, Scherer1986]
7. **Frequency code:** a slightly raised pitch softens a line (polite, cute, deferential, asking goodwill). A lowered
   pitch adds authority or threat. Use it for status shifts between characters (e.g., the young Ton vs. the old uncle
   Chan). [finding: Ohala1984, Gussenhoven2002]
8. **Bigger pitch excursion = "this matters".** Put the accent on new or contrastive words and de-accent repeated or
   given words. [finding: Gussenhoven2002, Cutler1997]
9. **There is no universal sarcasm voice.**
   - English: sarcasm often goes lower, flatter, noisier and slower.
   - Cantonese: it goes higher in F0.
   - Thai (one tiny study): it goes faster, higher, wider and louder.

   Direct sarcasm through intent and context, and A/B test both a "flat-low" and an "over-bright" reading in Thai.
   [finding: CheangPell2008, CheangPell2009, Kumwapee2020, BryantFoxTree2005, Rockwell2000]
10. **Non-verbal bursts are a separate, rich emotion channel** (24+ categories: sighs, gasps, "mm", laughs). Script
    and direct them separately from the words. Basic negative bursts travel across cultures, while many positive ones
    are culture-specific. [finding: Cowen2019, Sauter2010]
11. **Emotions blend along gradients** (anxiety → fear → horror; calm → awe). Transition and mixed-emotion directions
    are legitimate, and categories are better labels than valence/arousal numbers alone.
    [finding: CowenKeltner2017, Cowen2019]
12. **Cross-language decoding works but loses precision with cultural and linguistic distance.** Thai audiences are
    likely to read Thai-native expressive style best. Avoid importing exaggerated Western emotion templates into Thai
    VO. [finding: Pell2009, Scherer2001, LaukkaElfenbein2021, Laukka2016, ElfenbeinAmbady2002]
13. **In Thai, emotional F0 changes ride on top of five lexical tones.** Casual or weak-stress speech also shortens
    vowels and shifts some tones. Direct emotion through range, rate, loudness, voice quality, pauses and sentence-final
    particles rather than by "bending" individual syllables, and check that tones stay intelligible.
    [finding: TingsabadhAbramson1993] [craft]
14. **Acted emotion is partly a social display ("pull"), not just leakage ("push").** A stylised cartoon read and a
    restrained commercial read are both valid, and direction should state which display rules apply (genre, audience,
    politeness). [finding: BanseScherer1996, Scherer2003] [craft]
15. **To check a TTS take objectively,** measure F0 mean and range, loudness, rate and spectral slope/HNR with a GeMAPS
    set, and compare against the character's neutral baseline rather than absolute values.
    [finding: Eyben2016] [craft]
16. **Give every emotion two or three alternative readings** (e.g., grief as quiet-creaky or as broken-high). Studies
    show large within-emotion variation and several valid cue combinations. Pick by scene, not by table.
    [finding: BanzigerScherer2005, BryantFoxTree2005] [craft; Artistic]
